Algorithmic Encouragement

How Platforms Reward Risky & Illegal Behavior

The Viral Trap

How social media makes people do dangerous things for views.

Executive Summary

Modern social media platforms amplify content based on engagement rather than quality or safety. This creates an environment in which sensational, dangerous, and sometimes illegal behaviors are rewarded with visibility, monetization, and cultural influence.

The Problem

Apps like TikTok and Instagram care more about keeping you watching than keeping you safe. Because of this, they often show you people doing dangerous or illegal things because those videos get a lot of attention. This makes more people want to try the same dangerous stuff.

Algorithms reward emotional and extreme content. The app chooses shocking videos over safe ones.
Social contagion drives adolescent imitation. Teens copy what they see online to feel popular.
Dangerous challenges cause documented physical harm. Online challenges are actually hurting people.
Transparent algorithmic accountability is lacking. Big tech companies don't explain how they pick videos.

Understanding the Reward Cycle

Unlike censorship (removal), algorithmic encouragement is a reinforcement cycle that normalizes risk through feedback loops:

Why people copy dangerous things

It's a big circle of rewards that makes risky behavior feel normal:

1. Creator posts dangerous behavior Someone posts a video of them doing something risky.
2. Viewers are shocked → high engagement Lots of people watch, comment, and share because they are shocked.
3. Algorithm marks intensity and boosts reach The app sees the attention and shows the video to even more people.
4. Creator receives increased views and revenue The person who made the video gets famous and makes money.
5. Other creators imitate the trend to compete Now, everyone else tries the same dangerous thing to get famous too.

Social Contagion Mechanics

Social contagion describes how behaviors spread within networks, with adolescents being at significantly higher risk:

  • Higher Impulsivity: Weaker evaluation of long-term risk.
  • Social Validation: Increased need for peer approval and dopamine-driven attention.
  • Imitation Loops: Algorithmic marking of a trend leads to category-wide amplification.

Why Teens are at Risk

Dangerous trends spread like a virus, and teenagers are usually the first ones to "catch" it because:

  • Act First, Think Later: Their brains are still learning how to judge danger.
  • Wanting to Fit In: They care a lot about what their friends and the internet think of them.
  • FOMO: They don't want to miss out on the latest big trend.

Recommendations

These non-censorial interventions target promotion, design, and transparency rather than content removal.

How to Fix It

We don't need to ban videos. We just need to stop the apps from rewarding the bad ones.

Platform Design Improvements

Reduce recommendation weight for high-risk categories, add educational context overlays, and disable monetization for content showing high physical risk.

Change the Apps

The apps should stop recommending dangerous videos and stop letting people make money from them.

Policy & Transparency

Require transparency reports on harmful content amplification and mandate quicker responses to emergent threats.

Make Tech Companies be Honest

Force companies to show exactly how many people are seeing dangerous content and act faster when a new "challenge" starts hurting people.

Conclusion

Social media platforms are not neutral. By amplifying and monetizing dangerous content, they encourage risky behavior. Addressing this issue does not require censorship—only responsible platform design, transparent reporting, and accountability.

The Bottom Line

Social media isn't just a quiet observer—it's actively pushing people to do risky stuff for money and fame. We don't have to delete the internet to fix this; we just have to hold the tech companies responsible for what they choose to promote.
