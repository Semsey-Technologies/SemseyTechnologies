Abstract

Déjà vu is typically described as an anomalous feeling of familiarity without recollection, yet existing theories struggle to explain its precision, suddenness, and phenomenological clarity. This paper proposes a mechanistic account grounded in predictive processing: déjà vu arises when the brain’s pre‑experience simulations—generated during resting-state activity, dream-state generative modeling, or intrusive-thought micro-simulations—match incoming sensory patterns with high confidence but without an associated episodic memory trace.

The Short Version

Most people think Déjà Vu is a brain mistake. It's actually the opposite. Your brain is constantly "practicing" for the future while you sleep or rest. When real life perfectly matches one of those practices, you get that spooky feeling of familiarity. It's not a glitch—it's your brain working perfectly.

Introduction

Déjà vu—an intense sense of familiarity without recollection—has long resisted mechanistic explanation. Traditional accounts describe it as a memory error, temporal misalignment, or dual-processing glitch. Yet these interpretations fail to capture its precision, sudden onset, and striking phenomenological clarity.

This paper advances a predictive-processing account: déjà vu emerges when the brain’s pre-experience simulations, generated during resting-state cognition, dream-state generative activity, or intrusive-thought micro-simulation, align with present sensory input in the absence of an episodic memory trace.

What is Déjà Vu?

We’ve all had that feeling where we are in a new place or doing something new, but we swear we’ve done it before. Scientists used to think this was just a "short circuit" in the brain. But that doesn't explain why the feeling is so strong and specific.

This research suggests that Déjà Vu happens when the brain's "Future Guesser" (the part of your brain that predicts what happens next) gets it 100% right based on a "practice run" it did earlier.

Unified Architecture

These phenomena can be understood as different expressions of a single predictive system:

Phenomenon Bandwidth Function Contribution
Dreams High Broad generative exploration Large-scale simulations
Intrusive Thoughts Low Rapid micro-simulations Fine-grained fragments
Resting-State Medium Idle predictive rehearsal Environment templates

Where do the "Practice Runs" come from?

Your brain is always running simulations in the background. It uses three main ways to practice:

  • Dreams: Big, detailed stories where your brain tries out new situations.
  • Quick Thoughts: Those tiny "what if" thoughts you have during the day.
  • Daydreaming: When your brain is idle, it's actually rehearsing for common tasks.

When one of these practices matches real life, that's when you feel Déjà Vu.

Mechanism of the Déjà Vu Experience

1. Predictive Simulation

During rest, dreaming, or intrusive-thought activity, the brain generates scenario-like simulations encoding spatial layouts, social configurations, sensory patterns, and temporal sequences.

2. Real-World Alignment

Later, when sensory input resembles a prior simulation, the predictive system registers a high-confidence match.

3. Isolated Familiarity Signal

Because the simulation was never encoded as episodic memory, the system produces a familiarity signal without contextual recall.

How it works (Step-by-Step)

  1. The Practice: While you're sleeping or daydreaming, your brain builds a model of a room or a conversation.
  2. The Match: Weeks later, you walk into a room that looks exactly like your brain's model.
  3. The Feeling: Your brain says "Hey, I know this!" but because you don't remember the exact dream, it just feels like a weird, spooky sense of familiarity.

Conclusion

Déjà vu is not a malfunction but a window into predictive cognition. It arises when pre-experience simulations—generated during rest, dreaming, or intrusive-thought micro-simulation—match present sensory input without an episodic memory trace. This expanded model unifies familiarity, prediction, dreams, intrusive thoughts, and phenomenology under a single mechanism, reframing déjà vu as evidence of a continuously running anticipatory system that constructs experience before it occurs.

The Bottom Line

Déjà Vu isn't your brain breaking—it's your brain being too good at its job. It shows that your mind is constantly looking ahead and preparing you for what's coming next. When you feel it, it just means your brain's internal "forecast" was incredibly accurate.
